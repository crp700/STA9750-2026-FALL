Mini-Project #01: What We Watched: Exploring Netflix Viewing Data

Author

Calvin Ravito Putra

Introduction

For years, Netflix did not share how much its titles were watched. Since 2023, it has released a “What We Watched” report every six months, giving a rare look at viewing across nearly its entire catalog. This report uses the data from 2023 through mid-2026 to examine which titles draw the most viewing, how TV shows compare with movies, and how live events perform. It closes with a strategy memo recommending where Netflix should focus its content spending in 2027.

load_netflix_data <- function(){
  library(tidyverse)
  library(rvest)
  library(readxl)
  library(fs)
  
  data_dir <- path("data", "mp01")
    
  if(!dir_exists(data_dir)){
    dir_create(data_dir, showWarnings=FALSE, recurse=TRUE)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS <- list(
    `2023H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-a-netflix-engagement-report",
    `2023H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2023",
    `2024H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2024",
    `2024H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2024",
    `2025H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2025",
    `2025H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2025",
    `2026H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2026"
  )
  
  download_if_needed <- function(url, local){
    if(!file_exists(local)){
      download.file(url, local, quiet = TRUE, mode = "wb")
    }
    
    return(local)
  }
    
  NETFLIX_URLS |> 
    map(read_html) |> 
    map(html_element, xpath = "//a[contains(@href, '.xlsx')]") |> 
    map_chr(html_attr, "href") |> 
    enframe("period", "url") |> 
   mutate(path = path(data_dir, paste0(period, ".xlsx")), 
          path = map2(url, path, download_if_needed), 
          data = map(path, read_excel, skip=3, na="*"), 
          data = map(data, \(x) x |> mutate(`Release Date` = as.Date(`Release Date`)))) |>
    unnest(data) |> 
    select(-url, -path) |> 
    mutate(year=str_sub(period, 1, 4), 
           half=str_sub(period, start=6), 
           period_start = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 1, 7), 1), 
           period_end = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 6, 12), if_else(half==1, 30, 31))) |> 
    filter(!is.na(`Hours Viewed`)) |> 
    mutate(live_event = !is.na(Runtime) & str_detect(Runtime, "\\d+:\\d+\\*"), 
           clean_time = str_remove(Runtime, fixed("*")),
           Runtime = 60 * hour(hm(clean_time)) + minute(hm(clean_time))) |>
    select(-clean_time) |>
    mutate(Runtime = coalesce(Runtime, mean(Runtime, na.rm=TRUE)), 
           .by=Title, 
           Title = stringi::stri_trans_general(Title, "Latin-ASCII"),
           Title = str_remove(Title, " // .*"), 
           Title = str_remove(Title, "[^[:ascii:]].*"), 
           Title = str_trim(Title)) |>
    filter_out(Title == "") |>
    select(year, 
           period, 
           period_start, 
           period_end, 
           Title, 
           -half, 
           Runtime, 
           Views, 
           `Hours Viewed`, 
           `Release Date`,
           `Available Globally?`, 
           live_event)
}

After cleaning, glimpse() shows that every column has the right type: years are whole numbers, dates are dates, viewing numbers are numeric, and global availability and live events are TRUE/FALSE.

The skim() summary shows that a small number of titles get most of the attention. A typical title gets about 1.8 million hours of viewing in a period, but the average is much higher, around 8.3 million, because a few big hits pull it up. The biggest title reached 964 million hours.

Some information is missing. Runtime is missing for about 21% of rows, views for about 29% (the first report didn’t include views), and release date for more than half, so I’ll be careful when using release dates.

A few other things stand out. Only about 35% of titles are available worldwide, and live events make up just 0.5% of the data. There are 24,427 different titles across 61,892 rows, so many titles show up in more than one period. There are also some odd values, like a 2-minute runtime, a runtime of almost 400 hours, and a title that’s only one character long.

From the second half of 2023 onward, the catalog grows steadily, from about 6,600 titles to over 8,100, with total runtime rising along with it. The first period stands out, though: it has nearly three times as many titles, and most of them are missing a runtime.

The reason is that the later reports appear to include TV shows only. Starting with the second report, Netflix split its spreadsheet into separate tabs for TV and movies, and the data-loading code only reads the first tab. A quick check confirms this: KPop Demon Hunters, one of Netflix’s biggest movies of 2025, does not appear in the data at all. Only its lyric videos and bonus content do. This means comparisons across periods should be read with care, since the first period mixes shows and movies while the rest cover TV only.

Since the data doesn’t label titles as shows or movies, I sorted them with a simple rule: a title counts as a show if its name includes words like “Season” or “Series,” or if it runs longer than four hours. Titles with no runtime and no such keyword are marked as unknown.

The results mostly reflect the data problem found earlier. From the second half of 2023 onward, about 97-99% of viewing is classified as shows, and the small share of “movies” is likely short TV items such as specials or lyric videos rather than actual films. The first period is the only one that includes real movies, and most of them end up in the unknown group, because that report did not list runtimes. If we assume the unknown share there is mostly movies, then movies made up roughly a quarter of viewing in early 2023 and shows about three-quarters. Because later reports leave movies out, this data can’t tell us how that balance has changed since.

Because live events are much shorter than most shows, I compared them mainly using views, which adjusts for length. By that measure, a typical live event does better than a typical pre-recorded title: the median live event drew about 900,000 views, more than twice the 400,000 for pre-recorded content.

The picture changes at the top, though. The ten biggest pre-recorded titles averaged over 100 million views each, nearly ten times the top ten live events. So live events are a dependable way to draw a solid audience, but they haven’t produced the kind of massive hits that big series like Squid Game or Wednesday do. It’s also worth keeping in mind that there are only 310 live events in the data, so these results are less certain than those for pre-recorded content.

Strategy Memo

To: Netflix Senior Leadership

From: Calvin Ravito Putra

Subject: Where to focus original content spending in 2027

Bottom line: Most Netflix viewing comes from a small number of big, globally available series. For 2027, Netflix should spend more on fewer, larger global originals, cut back on the long tail of small titles, and grow live events carefully rather than quickly.

1. Bet on fewer, bigger hits. Viewing on Netflix is heavily concentrated. In the first half of 2026, the top 1% of titles captured 22.2% of all viewing, up from 19.8% in the second half of 2023. Meanwhile, the bottom half of the catalog accounts for only about 4.8% of viewing in a typical period. Over the same time, the catalog grew by 24%, from 6,595 to 8,187 titles. In other words, adding more titles is not adding much viewing, because most new titles end up in the long tail. The 2027 budget should move money away from many small productions and toward a handful of major series with the potential to become the next Squid Game or Wednesday, the kind of titles that top the charts each period.

2. Keep global originals at the core, but watch their edge. Globally available titles made up 38.2% of the catalog in the first half of 2026 but drew 49.1% of all viewing, about 1.3 times their share of titles. That advantage has narrowed, though: in the second half of 2023, global titles took 57.2% of viewing. Global originals should stay the center of the strategy, but Netflix should not neglect regional and licensed content. Older licensed shows like Breaking Bad and Suits are among the most consistently watched titles on the service, drawing steady viewing period after period without the risk of a new production.

3. Grow live events carefully. Netflix went from no live events before late 2024 to 0 in the second half of 2024 and 117 in the first half of 2026. The typical live event performs well, drawing 2.2 times the views of a typical pre-recorded title (900,000 vs. 400,000 median views). But live events still make up just 0.46% of total viewing, barely up from 0.00% in the second half of 2024. They also lack breakout hits: the top ten live events averaged 11,300,000 views, compared with 106,350,000 for the top ten pre-recorded titles. Live events offer steady, predictable audiences that advertisers value, but they are not hit-makers. Netflix should keep its current level of live programming and focus on the events with the biggest audiences, rather than expanding quickly.

A note on the data: After the first half of 2023, the reports used here cover TV only, not movies. These recommendations therefore apply to series and live programming. Movie strategy would need separate data.
